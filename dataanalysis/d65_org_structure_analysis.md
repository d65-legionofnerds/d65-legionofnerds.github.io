# The Org Chart That Ate Our Budget

**Legion of Data Nerds · Administrative Structure Series · April 2026**

*A structural history of District 65's administrative organization: how it was built, why it doubled, what the SDRP dismantled — and what architecture remains.*

*Based on FOIA documents, compensation reports, and board presentations*

---

District 65 is in a budget crisis. Enrollment has fallen. Counselors and librarians have been cut. Schools have been closed. And yet for years, the fastest-growing part of the district's budget wasn't classrooms or special education or transportation. It was the central office. Between fiscal years 2019 and 2026, student enrollment fell by 25% — from 7,943 students to 5,941 — while total district FTEs increased by at least 130, or roughly 10%. [[17][18]]

The headline numbers are stark. Between SY2015-16 and SY2025-26, K-8 enrollment fell from approximately 7,500 to 5,625 students — a 25% decline, the steepest among 21 nearby K-8 districts per Evanston RoundTable analysis. [RT1] Over that same period, administrative headcount roughly doubled, from 75 to 153 positions — a 104% increase. Total administrative compensation rose from about $10M to $20M in nominal dollars, and from $13.9M to $20.0M in inflation-adjusted 2026 dollars — a 44% real increase. Per-student administrative spending nearly tripled in nominal terms. [ADM] Perhaps most telling: per-administrator compensation has only kept pace with inflation over this period. The cost explosion was driven almost entirely by adding more administrators, not by paying existing ones more.

This analysis traces what happened to D65's administrative structure between 2016 and today — who was hired, why, at what cost, and what remains. The data comes from board-approved org charts [1,2,3], state compensation reports obtained through FOIA [7,8,9], SDRP board presentations [4,5], and the Legion's own staffing and budget analyses [ADM][16]. The picture is not flattering.

Counselors and librarians were eliminated — positions that exist to directly serve children and support the people who teach them. They were also, not coincidentally, easy targets: visible enough to signal sacrifice, small enough to leave the administrative apparatus untouched. The positions being cut served students. The structures driving costs remained protected. The SDRP has since made real cuts to the central office, but institutional bloat does not disappear just because the headcount drops. Understanding what was built, why, and what remains matters — because the community is being asked to trust that this administration will not repeat the same mistakes.

---

## 01 · Baseline — Where D65 Started: The 2016–17 Structure

Under Superintendent Paul Goren, District 65 operated with a relatively streamlined central office. The core leadership team consisted of eight senior positions covering the essential functions of a mid-size urban district. [1]

**2016–17 Central Administration (Goren Era):**

| Year | Headcount | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------|
| 2016–17 | 8 | Central admin positions |
| 2016–17 | 4 | Curriculum & Instruction directors |
| 2016–17 | 1 | Asst. Superintendent covering Student Services |

**Roles:** Superintendent · Asst. Supt. of Business Services · Chief Strategy Officer · Asst. Supt. of Schools · Asst. Supt. of HR · Asst. Supt. of Special Services · Chief Officer of Research & Data · Executive Director of Curriculum & Instruction [1]

Curriculum and instruction was managed by four directors — covering STEM, Literacy, ELL, and Social Studies. Operations were consolidated under business services. This was a functional structure for a district of D65's size and complexity.

It would not survive the next four years intact.

---

## 02 · Expansion — The Horton Era: When the Org Chart Doubled

Devon Horton arrived as superintendent in June 2020 with the MIRACLES framework — an eight-component reform agenda that required, by its own design logic, significant new administrative infrastructure. [10] The pandemic added further justification for expanded coordination capacity. [11]

What followed was one of the most rapid expansions of central office administration in the district's recent history.

**Administrative Growth: 2016–17 vs. 2020–22 Peak**

| Function | 2016–17 | 2020–22 Peak |
|----------|---------|--------------|
| Central Admin | 8 positions | 15+ positions |
| Curriculum & Instruction | 4 directors + 1 exec | 8+ directors + facilitators |
| Student Services / SPED | 1 asst. superintendent | 3+ directors under 1 asst. supt. |
| Technology / IT | embedded roles | 3+ dedicated positions |

The expansion was broad-based. A Deputy Superintendent position was created for Dr. LaTarsha Green. Seven assistant superintendents replaced the prior four or five. The curriculum and instruction division gained dedicated directors for MTSS & SEL, STEM, EvanSTEM, Literacy, and Social Sciences — plus four Content Facilitator positions. [2,3] New administrative units appeared for equity and family engagement, culture and climate, diversity hiring, and instructional technology. [6]

Critically, many of these additions were presented to the board as "budget neutral" — offset by eliminating other positions or reducing contracted services. [12] But the district was already projecting structural deficits starting in FY22. [13] The expansion happened anyway, justified by the MIRACLES framework and the superintendent's stated vision for organizational transformation.

> **From the Record:** Devon Horton told the Finance Committee in August 2020 that "as a new leader to the district, he made some hiring decisions to steer the vision." [14] Positions added during this period included an Equity Coach at $110,000, two Deans of Culture and Climate at a combined $196,858, and a Diversity Hiring Specialist at $95,500 [12] — all in a year when the district was already aware of an emerging structural deficit. [13]

The expansion accelerated further in SY2022-23 — Horton's first full year as superintendent. The IMRF support staff count jumped from 48 to 74 in a single year, accounting for the largest single-year increase in administrative compensation in the dataset: +$4.9M. New additions that year included a Family Center Managing Director, Director of Science, Diverse Learning Coordinator, Talent Development Coordinator, Executive Director of Technology, and multiple specialized coordinator positions. [ADM] Meanwhile, student enrollment was already trending downward. The ratio of administrators to students was climbing fast — and the academic results were not following. D65 math proficiency on the IAR stood at 44.8% in 2018 and had fallen to 42.3% by 2024. [RT1]

**Timeline of Events:**

- **June 2020** — Devon Horton arrives; MIRACLES framework announced. Deputy Superintendent created; assistant superintendent count grows; new equity, culture, and diversity roles added.
- **2020–2022** — Peak administrative expansion. Eight-plus curriculum directors; dedicated coordinators for technology, multilingual services, SEL, MTSS; four content facilitators added.
- **FY22** — Structural deficit projections emerge. District projects ongoing structural imbalance — the same fiscal year the expanded administrative structure reaches full cost.
- **2024–2025** — SDRP Phase I: 22 central office FTEs eliminated. Deputy Superintendent, two Chiefs of Academics, multiple directors and facilitators removed.
- **2025–2026** — SDRP Phase II: additional 21–26 FTEs proposed. Further consolidation; specialized director positions collapsed into Humanities and STEAM roles.
- **2025–26 / Today** — Current structure: leaner — but questions remain. Five senior leaders, two consolidated director positions — smaller than the 2016–17 baseline on paper, but per-pupil peer benchmarks still show D65 above peers on administrative spend.

## 03 · The SDRP Cuts — What the Structural Deficit Reduction Plan Actually Did

The SDRP achieved meaningful reductions in headcount. Across Phases I and II, the district eliminated at least 43–48 central office and administrative FTE positions, generating roughly $3.07 million in direct central office savings from 25 net FTE reductions in Phase II alone. [4,5]

**Key Numbers:**

| Metric | Figure |
|--------|--------|
| Central office FTEs eliminated in SDRP Phase I (FY25) | **22** |
| Additional FTEs reduced in SDRP Phase II (FY26) | **21–26** |
| Central office savings from Phase II alone | **$3.07M** |

The RoundTable documented a striking paradox in the FY26 data: the board approved 90 FTE reductions, yet budgeted salary and benefit expenses actually *increased* by approximately $700,000 compared to FY25 — from $132.6 million to $133.3 million. [18] Fewer bodies, higher aggregate cost. That is what happens when consolidation preserves high-compensation positions while eliminating lower-paid ones.

The eliminated positions read like a reversal of the Horton-era expansion: Deputy Superintendent, two Chiefs of Academics & School Management, Assistant Superintendent of Safety & Climate, Assistant Superintendent of Student Specialized Services, Director of Math, Director of Science, Director of Social Studies, Director of Training & Development, Director of Culture & Climate, four Content Facilitators. [5]

In curriculum and instruction, the eight-plus director structure was collapsed into two consolidated roles: Director of Humanities and Director of STEAM. [5][15] That is genuine organizational change, not just shuffling titles.

**Before & After: Central Admin Senior Positions**

| Era | Senior Positions |
|-----|-----------------|
| 2016–17 (Goren baseline) | 8 |
| 2020–22 (Horton-era peak) | 15+ |
| 2025–26 (post-SDRP current) | 5 |

## 04 · What Remains — The Structure That Survived the Cuts

Headcount reduction is not the same as cost reduction, and organizational restructuring does not guarantee institutional reform. Three issues deserve scrutiny in the current structure.

### 1. Executive Compensation Remains High

The four top administrative positions — even after the cuts — carry combined compensation exceeding $924,000 annually. [7] This in a district that eliminated counselors and librarians and is now asking staff to absorb cuts.

**FY26 Senior Administrator Total Compensation** (Source: PA 96-0434 TRS Administrator Salary Report FY26 [7])

| Name | Title | Compensation |
|------|-------|-------------|
| Dr. Angel Turner | Superintendent | $285,714 |
| Tamara Mitchell | Chief Financial Officer | $238,417 |
| Dr. Stacy Beardsley | Asst. Supt. of Accountability | $206,092 |
| Dr. Kathleen Speth | Asst. Supt. of Academics | $194,285 |
| Director of Humanities | Consolidated curriculum role | $150,417 |
| Director of STEAM | Consolidated curriculum role | $142,857 |

### 2. Administrative Support Positions Stay Above $75K

The IMRF compensation report — which captures non-TRS administrative and support staff earning over $75,000 — shows a substantial layer of positions below the senior leadership team that the SDRP largely left intact. Communications Manager at $108,145. Infrastructure Manager at $135,345. Multiple administrative assistants and specialists above $75,000. [8]

This is not inherently wrong — some of these are specialized technical roles. But it is the layer of the organization that received the least scrutiny under the SDRP, and it is where comparison to peer districts would be most revealing.

The RoundTable's March 2026 analysis puts numbers to this layer. Using the three state disclosure lists together, Larry Gavin identified **80 employees at the JEH administration building holding administrative, director, manager, coordinator, or chief titles** — out of a total of 200 people working at JEH. [18] That is a substantial administrative superstructure for a district that now educates roughly 5,900 students.

### 3. Special Area Administration Remains a Peer-Comparison Problem

Our earlier peer budget analysis flagged Function 2490 (Special Area Administration) as one of D65's most significant outliers: $619 per pupil versus a peer average of $176. That is 3.5 times the peer average. Northbrook manages at $355. Wilmette at $144. [16]

> **The Core Issue:** The SDRP reduced the number of bodies in the central office and genuinely flattened the org chart. What it did not resolve is the structural question of whether D65's remaining administrative architecture — its functions, spans of control, and cost-per-pupil — is calibrated to actual student need — or simply to historical practice and accumulated organizational commitments. Until the district can show peer-comparable per-pupil administrative spending, the question of institutional bloat remains open.

> **On Student Achievement:** The RoundTable noted that higher staff-per-student ratios have not produced better outcomes. On the 2018 state assessment, 44.8% of D65 students met the math proficiency benchmark. By 2024, that figure had fallen to 42.3%. [18] A district with more administrators per pupil and declining academic results carries a heavy burden of proof when arguing that the current staffing model is structured around student outcomes.

Community Services (Function 3000) remains a separate concern. Our peer analysis shows D65 spending 14 times the peer average per pupil on this function — $1,092 per pupil versus a peer average of $74, with no peer district coming close. [16] The SDRP presentations did not address this line explicitly, and the district has not provided a public breakdown of what sits there.

### 4. Student-Facing Cuts vs. Administrative Right-Sizing

The contrast between what was cut and what was protected is sharpest when you put the numbers side by side. The April 2026 layoff of two middle-school counselors is projected to save approximately $200,000 annually. The reassignment of all three middle-school librarians — eliminating the librarian function from Chute, Haven, and Nichols — saved approximately zero in direct salary costs, since the staff were reassigned rather than terminated. Combined, these were the most publicly visible and contested cuts of the 2025-26 cycle. [ADM]

By contrast, returning D65's administrative headcount to its 2015-16 level of 75 positions would represent a reduction of roughly 78 positions from the current 153 — with savings that would dwarf the counselor and librarian actions many times over. Even a more modest scenario — trimming to a headcount consistent with peer-district ratios — would free up resources at a scale the district has not yet pursued. [ADM] The board has direct leverage here through the superintendent evaluation and organizational structure decisions. That lever has not been pulled to its full extent.

## 05 · The Argument — What This Means for the Budget Debate

The SDRP narrative is that the district has done the hard work of administrative restructuring — that the org chart has been rationalized and the structural problems addressed. The numbers support that claim — partially. Real positions were eliminated. Real savings were achieved. The current leadership structure is smaller than at any point since 2016.

But three things remain true simultaneously:

**First**, the expansion that required the cuts was foreseeable. The district was already projecting structural deficits in FY22 [13] while adding equity coaches, culture deans, and deputy superintendents. [12] The community was not clearly informed about the tradeoffs being made in real time.

**Second**, the peer benchmarks have not closed. D65 still spends significantly more per pupil on central administration than comparable districts. Fewer positions at higher individual salaries can produce the same or higher aggregate cost. The consolidated directors alone cost $293,000 combined — approximately what four specialized directors might have cost individually when spread across the prior structure. And the RoundTable's analysis of all three state disclosure lists found that cross-referencing them yields a count of 62 administrators for FY26 — 10 higher than the 52 the district reported under PA 96-0434 alone — raising the question of how many employees are performing administrative functions without being formally classified as administrators. [18]

**Third**, the cuts that generated community backlash — counselors, librarians, instructional support — were student-facing positions. The administrative apparatus absorbed a significant share of the reductions, but the visible, emotionally resonant cuts fell disproportionately on roles that directly serve children. And crucially, the administrative buildup has not produced the academic outcomes its hiring rationale would presumably have promised: D65 math proficiency on the state IAR assessment stood at 44.8% in 2018 and had declined to 42.3% by 2024, through a period of significant central office expansion. [RT1]

> *The district eliminated administrators and student-facing staff in the same sweep. Those are not equivalent choices, and they should not be discussed as if they are.*

The administration deserves credit for the structural changes made under the SDRP. The org chart is meaningfully different from its 2021 peak. But a restructured organization is not automatically a well-structured one. The remaining administrative architecture — who sits at JEH, what functions they perform, and at what cost per pupil — deserves the same rigor the board applied to school buildings.

The Legion of Data Nerds will continue tracking per-pupil administrative spending against the peer cohort and monitoring the FY27 budget for whether the consolidation narrative holds under financial pressure.

## Methodology

This analysis draws on board-approved organizational charts (2016–17, 2020–21, 2021–22, and 2025–26), state-required administrator salary disclosures (PA 96-0434 TRS and PA 97-0609 IMRF, FY26), SDRP board presentations (January and May 2025), and ISBE functional expenditure data used in our peer-district budget comparison. Primary documents were retrieved and organized using [FOIA Gras](https://foiagras.com), a public records research platform that has assembled an Inspector General-style document library of D65 board materials obtained through FOIA. The Legion of Data Nerds relies on FOIA Gras as a core research infrastructure tool for this work.

Document analysis and thematic synthesis were supported by [Claude](https://claude.ai) (Anthropic), an AI assistant used to identify patterns across compensation reports, org charts, and board presentations, and to draft narrative text from the source material. All factual claims were verified against the underlying primary documents by Legion members before publication.

Peer comparison figures reference the cohort established in our prior budget analysis: Oak Park District 97, Wilmette District 39, Northbrook District 28, Glenview, Park Ridge, and Wheeling, among others. Per-pupil expenditure data are sourced from ISBE Annual Financial Reports (AFR) filed through the ISBE Cerberus database, with enrollment figures from the Illinois Report Card. Full methodology for the peer comparison is available at [d65-legionofnerds.github.io/dataanalysis/budget_analysis.html](https://d65-legionofnerds.github.io/dataanalysis/budget_analysis.html).

Administrative staffing and compensation trends are documented in detail at [d65-legionofnerds.github.io/dataanalysis/admin-growth.html](https://d65-legionofnerds.github.io/dataanalysis/admin-growth.html), which tracks headcount and total compensation from SY2015-16 through SY2025-26 using the same PA 96-0434 and PA 97-0609 disclosures. Reporting by Larry Gavin in the *Evanston RoundTable* — particularly his March 2026 analysis and November 2025 guest essay — provided independent corroboration of enrollment decline trends and peer comparisons. Inline citations throughout this post are keyed to the source appendix below.
